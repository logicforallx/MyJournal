Law of Identity: A statement is identical to itself. Example: "The cat is on the mat." (The statement is identical to itself; it is true.)

Law of Non-Contradiction: A statement cannot be both true and false at the same time. Example: "The cat is on the mat, and the cat is not on the mat." (This statement contradicts itself and is therefore false.)

Law of Excluded Middle: A statement must be either true or false; there is no middle ground. Example: "The cat is either on the mat or not on the mat." (This statement asserts that there are no other possibilities; it is true.)

Law of Double Negation: If a statement is negated twice, it is equivalent to the original statement. Example: "It is not true that the cat is not on the mat." (This statement is equivalent to saying "The cat is on the mat.")

Law of Contraposition: If a conditional statement is true, its contrapositive is also true. Example: "If it is raining, then the ground is wet." (The contrapositive: "If the ground is not wet, then it is not raining.")

Law of Modus Ponens: If the antecedent of a conditional statement is true, then the consequent must also be true. Example: If "If it is raining, then the ground is wet" and "It is raining" are true, then "The ground is wet" must also be true.

Law of Modus Tollens: If the consequent of a conditional statement is false, then the antecedent must also be false. Example: If "If it is raining, then the ground is wet" is true and "The ground is not wet" is true, then "It is not raining" must also be true.

Law of Syllogism: If two conditional statements are linked by sharing a common term, then a conclusion can be drawn by combining the antecedent of the first statement and the consequent of the second statement. Example: If "All humans are mortal" and "Socrates is a human" are true, then we can conclude that "Socrates is mortal."

Law of Addition: If a statement is true, then the disjunction (OR) of that statement with any other statement is also true. Example: If "It is raining" is true, then "It is raining or the sun is shining" is also true.

Law of Simplification: If a conjunction (AND) of two statements is true, then each individual statement is also true. Example: If "It is raining and the ground is wet" is true, then "It is raining" and "The ground is wet" are both true.

Law of Composition: If two statements are true, then their conjunction (AND) is also true. Example: If "The sky is blue" and "The grass is green" are both true, then "The sky is blue and the grass is green" is also true.

Law of Distribution: If a disjunction (OR) is true and one of its disjuncts is true, then the disjunction of that disjunct with another statement is also true. Example: If "It is raining or the ground is wet" is true and "It is raining" is true, then "It is raining or the ground is wet or the sun is shining" is also true.

Law of Transitivity: If a first statement implies a second statement, and the second statement implies a third statement, then the first statement implies the third statement. Example: If "All dogs are mammals" implies "All mammals are animals," and "All mammals are animals" implies "All animals are living beings," then "All dogs are living beings" is true.

Principle of Induction: If a certain property or characteristic is observed in a sample of a group, it is reasonable to infer that the same property or characteristic holds for the entire group. Example: If a survey finds that 80% of participants prefer chocolate ice cream, it is reasonable to infer that approximately 80% of the population as a whole prefers chocolate ice cream.
