### 1. **Propositions and Truth Values**

A **proposition** is a statement that is either **true** or **false**, but not both. These are the basic building blocks of formal logic.

#### Examples of Propositions:
- "The sky is blue." (This is a proposition because it can be true or false.)
- "2 + 2 = 4." (This is a proposition because it is true.)
- "The moon is made of cheese." (This is a proposition because it is false.)

#### Non-examples of Propositions:
- "What time is it?" (This is a question, not a proposition.)
- "Close the door." (This is a command, not a proposition.)

#### **Truth Values**:
- Every proposition has a **truth value**:
  - **True** (denoted as T or 1).
  - **False** (denoted as F or 0).

Let's go over **Simple vs. Compound Propositions** before we move forward. Thanks for pointing that out!

#### **Simple Propositions**
A **simple proposition** (or atomic proposition) is a statement that cannot be broken down into smaller parts. It expresses a single idea or fact.

##### Examples:
- "The sun is a star."
- "2 + 2 = 4."
- "Water is wet."

These are all simple propositions because they represent a single fact or idea and don't have any logical connectors like "and," "or," or "if."

#### **Compound Propositions**
A **compound proposition** is formed by combining two or more simple propositions using **logical operators** (connectives), such as "and," "or," "not," and "if...then."

##### Examples:
- "The sun is a star **and** water is wet."
- "If it rains, **then** the ground will be wet."
- "2 + 2 = 4 **or** 3 + 3 = 7."
- "It is **not** true that 2 + 2 = 5."

Each of these is a compound proposition because they involve two or more simple propositions linked by logical connectives.

#### Breaking It Down:

1. **Simple Proposition**: "The sky is blue."
2. **Simple Proposition**: "It is raining."
3. **Compound Proposition**: "The sky is blue **and** it is raining."

The compound proposition combines two simple ones using "and."

### **2. Logical Operators (Connectives)**

Logical operators connect propositions to form compound propositions. Here are the most common operators:

#### **Negation (¬)**
- **Symbol**: ¬
- **Meaning**: Not
- **Example**: If \( P \) is "It is raining," then ¬\( P \) is "It is not raining."

#### **Conjunction (∧)**
- **Symbol**: ∧
- **Meaning**: And
- **Example**: \( P \) ∧ \( Q \) means "P and Q." If \( P \) is "It is raining" and \( Q \) is "It is cold," then \( P \) ∧ \( Q \) is "It is raining and it is cold."

#### **Disjunction (∨)**
- **Symbol**: ∨
- **Meaning**: Or
- **Example**: \( P \) ∨ \( Q \) means "P or Q." If \( P \) is "It is raining" and \( Q \) is "It is sunny," then \( P \) ∨ \( Q \) is "It is raining or it is sunny."

#### **Implication (→)**
- **Symbol**: →
- **Meaning**: If...then
- **Example**: \( P \) → \( Q \) means "If P, then Q." If \( P \) is "It is raining" and \( Q \) is "The ground is wet," then \( P \) → \( Q \) is "If it is raining, then the ground is wet."

#### **Biconditional (↔)**
- **Symbol**: ↔
- **Meaning**: If and only if
- **Example**: \( P \) ↔ \( Q \) means "P if and only if Q." If \( P \) is "It is raining" and \( Q \) is "The ground is wet," then \( P \) ↔ \( Q \) is "It is raining if and only if the ground is wet."

### **3. Truth Tables**

Truth tables are used to determine the truth value of compound propositions based on the truth values of their components. They show how the truth value of the compound proposition depends on the truth values of its simpler propositions.

#### **Basic Truth Table Structure**

Let's construct truth tables for each of the basic logical operators using propositions \( P \) and \( Q \).

**a. Negation (¬)**

| \( P \) | ¬\( P \) |
|---|---|
| T | F |
| F | T |

**b. Conjunction (∧)**

| \( P \) | \( Q \) | \( P \) ∧ \( Q \) |
|---|---|---|
| T | T | T |
| T | F | F |
| F | T | F |
| F | F | F |

**c. Disjunction (∨)**

| \( P \) | \( Q \) | \( P \) ∨ \( Q \) |
|---|---|---|
| T | T | T |
| T | F | T |
| F | T | T |
| F | F | F |

**d. Implication (→)**

The implication \( P \to Q \) (read as "If P, then Q") is true in all cases except when P is true and Q is false. Here's why:

- **If P is true and Q is true**, then \( P \to Q \) is true because the condition is satisfied.
- **If P is true and Q is false**, then \( P \to Q \) is false because the condition is not satisfied.
- **If P is false**, \( P \to Q \) is true regardless of Q's value. This is because an implication with a false antecedent (P) is always considered true (i.e., a false statement implies anything).

*Truth Table for Implication (→):*

| \( P \) | \( Q \) | \( P \to Q \) | Explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | T | If it's raining (P) and the ground is wet (Q), then the statement "If it rains, then the ground will be wet" is true. |
| T | F | F | If it's raining (P) and the ground is not wet (Q), then the statement "If it rains, then the ground will be wet" is false. |
| F | T | T | If it's not raining (P) and the ground is wet (Q), the statement "If it rains, then the ground will be wet" is true (a false premise implies anything). |
| F | F | T | If it's not raining (P) and the ground is not wet (Q), the statement "If it rains, then the ground will be wet" is still true (a false premise implies anything). |

**e. Biconditional (↔)**


The biconditional \( P \leftrightarrow Q \) (read as "P if and only if Q") is true when both propositions have the same truth value. It’s false when the truth values of P and Q differ.

- **If P and Q are both true**, \( P \leftrightarrow Q \) is true because both sides of the equivalence are true.
- **If P and Q are both false**, \( P \leftrightarrow Q \) is true because both sides of the equivalence are false.
- **If P is true and Q is false**, \( P \leftrightarrow Q \) is false because the truth values are different.
- **If P is false and Q is true**, \( P \leftrightarrow Q \) is false because the truth values are different.

*Truth Table for Biconditional (↔):*

| \( P \) | \( Q \) | \( P \leftrightarrow Q \) | Explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | T | If it's raining (P) and the ground is wet (Q), the statement "It is raining if and only if the ground is wet" is true, because both are true. |
| T | F | F | If it's raining (P) and the ground is not wet (Q), the statement "It is raining if and only if the ground is wet" is false, because one is true and the other is false. |
| F | T | F | If it's not raining (P) and the ground is wet (Q), the statement "It is raining if and only if the ground is wet" is false, because one is false and the other is true. |
| F | F | T | If it's not raining (P) and the ground is not wet (Q), the statement "It is raining if and only if the ground is wet" is true, because both are false. |

**Constructing a Truth Table**

To construct a truth table:
1. List all possible truth values for the propositions.
2. Apply the logical operators according to the compound proposition you are analyzing.
3. Record the result for each combination of truth values.

### **4. Logical Equivalences**

Logical equivalence is when two statements have the same truth value in all possible scenarios. This means that one statement can be transformed into another without changing its truth value. There are several important laws and equivalences used to simplify and manipulate logical expressions.

#### **Common Logical Equivalences**

1. **Identity Laws**
   - **\( P \) ∧ True** = \( P \)
   - **\( P \) ∨ False** = \( P \)
   - **Explanation**: Conjunction with True or disjunction with False doesn’t change the value of \( P \).

2. **Domination Laws**
   - **\( P \) ∧ False** = False
   - **\( P \) ∨ True** = True
   - **Explanation**: Conjunction with False or disjunction with True results in the dominating value (False or True, respectively).

3. **Double Negation Law**
   - **¬(¬\( P \))** = \( P \)
   - **Explanation**: Negating a negation returns the original value.

4. **De Morgan’s Laws**
   - **¬(\( P \) ∧ \( Q \))** = (¬\( P \)) ∨ (¬\( Q \))
   - **¬(\( P \) ∨ \( Q \))** = (¬\( P \)) ∧ (¬\( Q \))
   - **Explanation**: Negation distributes over conjunction and disjunction, swapping ∧ with ∨ and vice versa.

5. **Distributive Laws**
   - **\( P \) ∧ (\( Q \) ∨ \( R \))** = (\( P \) ∧ \( Q \)) ∨ (\( P \) ∧ \( R \))
   - **\( P \) ∨ (\( Q \) ∧ \( R \))** = (\( P \) ∨ \( Q \)) ∧ (\( P \) ∨ \( R \))
   - **Explanation**: Distributes ∧ over ∨ and vice versa.

6. **Absorption Laws**
   - **\( P \) ∨ (\( P \) ∧ \( Q \))** = \( P \)
   - **\( P \) ∧ (\( P \) ∨ \( Q \))** = \( P \)
   - **Explanation**: One part absorbs the other in disjunction or conjunction.

7. **Commutative Laws**
   - **\( P \) ∧ \( Q \)** = \( Q \) ∧ \( P \)
   - **\( P \) ∨ \( Q \)** = \( Q \) ∨ \( P \)
   - **Explanation**: The order of propositions does not affect the outcome of ∧ or ∨.

8. **Associative Laws**
   - **(\( P \) ∧ (\( Q \) ∧ \( R \)))** = ((\( P \) ∧ \( Q \)) ∧ \( R \))
   - **(\( P \) ∨ (\( Q \) ∨ \( R \)))** = ((\( P \) ∨ \( Q \)) ∨ \( R \))
   - **Explanation**: Grouping of propositions in ∧ or ∨ does not affect the outcome.

9. **Implication Equivalence**
   - **\( P \to Q \)** = **¬\( P \) ∨ \( Q \)**
   - **Explanation**: An implication can be expressed as a disjunction where the antecedent is negated.

#### **Examples of Applying Logical Equivalences**

1. **Simplifying Expressions**
   - Simplify **¬(P ∧ Q)**
     - Using De Morgan’s Law: **¬(P ∧ Q)** = **¬P ∨ ¬Q**

2. **Proving Equivalences**
   - Prove **P ∨ (P ∧ Q)** = **P**
     - By Absorption Law: **P ∨ (P ∧ Q)** = **P**

3. **Transforming Implications**
   - Transform **P → (Q ∧ R)**
     - Using Implication Equivalence: **P → (Q ∧ R)** = **¬P ∨ (Q ∧ R)**

4. **Using Distributive Law**
   - Simplify **P ∧ (Q ∨ R)**
     - By Distributive Law: **P ∧ (Q ∨ R)** = **(P ∧ Q) ∨ (P ∧ R)**

#### **Practice Problems**

1. **Simplify** the expression **¬(P ∨ ¬Q) ∧ (R ∨ Q)**.
2. **Prove** that **(P ∨ Q) ∧ (P ∨ R)** is logically equivalent to **P ∨ (Q ∧ R)**.

### **5. Rules of Inference**

Rules of inference are logical rules that dictate the valid steps for deriving conclusions from premises. Here are some of the key rules:

#### **1. Modus Ponens (MP)**

**Form**:
- Premise 1: \( P \to Q \) (If \( P \), then \( Q \))
- Premise 2: \( P \) ( \( P \) is true)
- Conclusion: \( Q \) (Therefore, \( Q \) must be true)

**Example**:
- Premise 1: If it rains, the ground will be wet.
- Premise 2: It is raining.
- Conclusion: The ground is wet.

#### **2. Modus Tollens (MT)**

**Form**:
- Premise 1: \( P \to Q \) (If \( P \), then \( Q \))
- Premise 2: \( \neg Q \) ( \( Q \) is false)
- Conclusion: \( \neg P \) (Therefore, \( P \) must be false)

**Example**:
- Premise 1: If it rains, the ground will be wet.
- Premise 2: The ground is not wet.
- Conclusion: It is not raining.

#### **3. Hypothetical Syllogism (HS)**

**Form**:
- Premise 1: \( P \to Q \) (If \( P \), then \( Q \))
- Premise 2: \( Q \to R \) (If \( Q \), then \( R \))
- Conclusion: \( P \to R \) (Therefore, if \( P \), then \( R \))

**Example**:
- Premise 1: If it rains, the ground will be wet.
- Premise 2: If the ground is wet, the plants will grow.
- Conclusion: If it rains, the plants will grow.

#### **4. Disjunctive Syllogism (DS)**

**Form**:
- Premise 1: \( P \lor Q \) ( \( P \) or \( Q \))
- Premise 2: \( \neg P \) ( \( P \) is false)
- Conclusion: \( Q \) (Therefore, \( Q \) must be true)

**Example**:
- Premise 1: Either it is raining or it is sunny.
- Premise 2: It is not raining.
- Conclusion: It is sunny.

#### **5. Conjunction (Conj)**

**Form**:
- Premise 1: \( P \) ( \( P \) is true)
- Premise 2: \( Q \) ( \( Q \) is true)
- Conclusion: \( P \land Q \) (Therefore, \( P \) and \( Q \) are both true)

**Example**:
- Premise 1: It is raining.
- Premise 2: The ground is wet.
- Conclusion: It is raining and the ground is wet.

#### **6. Simplification (Simp)**

**Form**:
- Premise: \( P \land Q \) ( \( P \) and \( Q \) are both true)
- Conclusion: \( P \) (Therefore, \( P \) is true)

**Example**:
- Premise: It is raining and the ground is wet.
- Conclusion: It is raining.

#### **7. Addition (Add)**

**Form**:
- Premise: \( P \) ( \( P \) is true)
- Conclusion: \( P \lor Q \) (Therefore, \( P \) or \( Q \) is true)

**Example**:
- Premise: It is raining.
- Conclusion: It is raining or it is sunny.
