#  Fundamental laws & Equivalences

Let’s go through **ALL fundamental laws** of logic, including **logical equivalences**, each with a natural, relatable example to make them clearer.

---

### **Fundamental Laws of Logic**:

### 1. **Law of Identity** 
   - **Statement**: A proposition is identical to itself.
   - **Formal Expression**: \( P \equiv P \)
   - **Example**: "The sky is blue" is the same as saying "The sky is blue." This is a tautology (true in all cases).

### 2. **Law of Non-Contradiction**
   - **Statement**: A proposition cannot be both true and false at the same time.
   - **Formal Expression**: \( \neg(P \land \neg P) \)
   - **Example**: "The door is open" cannot be both true and false at the same time.

### 3. **Law of Excluded Middle**
   - **Statement**: A proposition is either true or false; there is no middle ground.
   - **Formal Expression**: \( P \lor \neg P \)
   - **Example**: Either "The light is on" is true, or "The light is not on" is true.
   - **Example**: Either you are inside the house or you are not; there’s no "in-between" state.

### 4. **Law of Double Negation**
   - **Statement**: A double negation of a proposition is equivalent to the proposition itself.
   - **Formal Expression**: \( \neg(\neg P) \equiv P \)
   - **Example**: If it's not false that "The cat is on the mat," then "The cat is on the mat" must be true.
   - **Example**: If it’s not true that "I did not eat lunch," then it means "I ate lunch."

### **Logical Equivalences (Laws of Logic)**:

These equivalences describe how logical expressions can be transformed or simplified.

### 1. **Commutative Laws** (order of propositions doesn't matter)
   - **Conjunction**: \( P \land Q \equiv Q \land P \)
   - **Disjunction**: \( P \lor Q \equiv Q \lor P \)
   - **Example (Conjunction)**: "The sky is blue and the grass is green" is the same as "The grass is green and the sky is blue."
   - **Example**: Whether you say "I am hungry and tired" or "I am tired and hungry," the meaning doesn't change.
   
### 2. **Associative Laws** (grouping doesn't matter)
   - **Conjunction**: \( (P \land Q) \land R \equiv P \land (Q \land R) \)
   - **Disjunction**: \( (P \lor Q) \lor R \equiv P \lor (Q \lor R) \)
   - **Example (Disjunction)**: "It will rain or it will snow, or it will be windy" is the same as "It will rain, or it will snow or be windy."
   - **Natural Example**: Grouping items in "I want an apple or (an orange or a banana)" is the same as "I want (an apple or an orange) or a banana."

### 3. **Distributive Laws** (distributing across conjunctions/disjunctions)
   - **Conjunction over Disjunction**: \( P \land (Q \lor R) \equiv (P \land Q) \lor (P \land R) \)
   - **Disjunction over Conjunction**: \( P \lor (Q \land R) \equiv (P \lor Q) \land (P \lor R) \)
   - **Example (Conjunction over Disjunction)**: "I will go to the park if it’s sunny or if it’s warm" is the same as saying "If it’s sunny, I will go to the park, or if it’s warm, I will go to the park."
   - **Natural Example**: "I will eat pizza or salad, and drink water" becomes "I will eat pizza and drink water, or I will eat salad and drink water."

### 4. **De Morgan's Laws** (negation distributes)
   - **Negation of a Conjunction**: \( \neg(P \land Q) \equiv \neg P \lor \neg Q \)
   - **Negation of a Disjunction**: \( \neg(P \lor Q) \equiv \neg P \land \neg Q \)
   - **Example (Negation of Conjunction)**: "It is not true that both the cat and dog are outside" is the same as "Either the cat is not outside or the dog is not outside."
   - **Natural Example**: "It’s not true that both Alice and Bob are coming" means "Either Alice is not coming or Bob is not coming."

### 5. **Idempotent Laws** (repeating doesn't change the outcome)
   - **Conjunction**: \( P \land P \equiv P \)
   - **Disjunction**: \( P \lor P \equiv P \)
   - **Example (Conjunction)**: "It is raining and it is raining" is logically equivalent to "It is raining."
   - **Natural Example**: "I will eat pizza and pizza" is the same as "I will eat pizza."

### 6. **Absorption Laws** (one part absorbs the other)
   - **Conjunction**: \( P \land (P \lor Q) \equiv P \)
   - **Disjunction**: \( P \lor (P \land Q) \equiv P \)
   - **Example (Conjunction)**: "It’s sunny and it’s either sunny or warm" simplifies to "It’s sunny."
   - **Natural Example**: "I will exercise and either exercise or sleep" simplifies to "I will exercise."

### 7. **Identity Laws** (identity elements for AND/OR)
   - **Conjunction**: \( P \land \text{True} \equiv P \)
   - **Disjunction**: \( P \lor \text{False} \equiv P \)
   - **Example (Conjunction)**: "It’s raining and that’s true" is the same as saying "It’s raining."
   - **Natural Example**: "I will go to the gym, and it’s true" simplifies to "I will go to the gym."

### 8. **Domination Laws** (dominating elements for AND/OR)
   - **Conjunction**: \( P \land \text{False} \equiv \text{False} \)
   - **Disjunction**: \( P \lor \text{True} \equiv \text{True} \)
   - **Example (Disjunction)**: "I will either go to the park or something true" simplifies to "True" (it’s always true).
   - **Natural Example**: "I will eat either food or nothing" simplifies to "I will eat."

### 9. **Negation Laws** (negating results in opposites)
   - **Conjunction**: \( P \land \neg P \equiv \text{False} \)
   - **Disjunction**: \( P \lor \neg P \equiv \text{True} \)
   - **Example (Conjunction)**: "It is raining and it is not raining" is always false.
   - **Natural Example**: "I will go to the park and not go to the park" is false.

### 10. **Implication Laws** (implication to disjunction)
   - **Implication**: \( P \to Q \equiv \neg P \lor Q \)
   - **Example**: "If it rains, the grass will be wet" is the same as "Either it does not rain, or the grass will be wet."
   - **Natural Example**: "If I study, I will pass the exam" becomes "Either I won’t study, or I will pass the exam."

### 11. **Contrapositive Law** (contrapositive equivalence)
   - **Implication**: \( P \to Q \equiv \neg Q \to \neg P \)
   - **Example**: "If the grass is wet, it rained" is logically equivalent to "If it didn’t rain, the grass is not wet."
   - **Natural Example**: "If I am hungry, I will eat" becomes "If I didn’t eat, then I am not hungry."
