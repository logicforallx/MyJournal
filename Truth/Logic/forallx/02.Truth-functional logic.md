# 4. First steps to symbolization

## 4.1 Validity in virtue of form

* The validity of the arguments just considered has nothing very much to do with the meanings of English expressions. If it has to do with meanings at all, it is with the meanings of phrases like ‘and’, ‘or’, ‘not,’ and ‘if …, then …’.

* A **truth-functional logic, or TFL** is a formal language which allows us to symbolize many arguments in such a way as to show that they are *valid in virtue of their form.*
    - Therefore, valid argument here define as valid in good quality of their formation or form. 

## 4.2 Validity for special reasons

* There are plenty of arguments that are valid, but not for reasons relating to their form. Take an example:
        
        ​Juanita is a vixen.
        ∴​Juanita is a fox.

* It is impossible for the premise to be true and the conclusion false, since ‘vixen’ just means ‘female fox’. So the argument is (conceptually) valid. The validity is not explained by the form of the argument.

**TFL will help us to understand arguments that are valid due to their form.**

## 4.3 Atomic sentences

* We will use single uppercase letters as sentence letters of TFL.

* There are only twenty-six letters of the alphabet, but there is no limit to the number of sentence letters that we might want to consider. By adding subscripts to letters, we obtain new sentence letters. So, here are five different sentence letters of TFL: \( A, P, P_1, P_2, A_{234},.. \)

* We will use sentence letters to represent, or symbolize, certain English sentences. To do this, we provide a symbolization key , such as the following:
    - \(A\): It is raining outside
    - \(C\): It is raining outside

## 5.1 Negation

**Negation (NOT)**: \( \neg \) or \( \sim \)  

A sentence can be symbolized as \( \neg A \) if it can be paraphrased in English as ‘It is not the case that…’.
   - Example: \( \neg P \) (Not P)

Consider how we might symbolize these sentences:

1. ​Mary is in Barcelona.
    - \(B\): Mary is in Barcelona.
2. ​It is not the case that Mary is in Barcelona.
    - \( \neg B \)
3. ​Mary is not in Barcelona.
    - \( \neg B \)
4. ​The widget can be replaced.
    - \(R\): The widget is replaceable
5. ​The widget is irreplaceable.
    - \( \neg R \)
6. ​The widget is not irreplaceable.
    - \( \neg \neg R \)

But some care is needed when handling negations. Consider:

7. ​Jane is happy.
8. ​Jane is unhappy.

If we let the TFL-sentence ‘\(H\)’ symbolize ‘Jane is happy’, then we can symbolize sentence 7 as ‘\(H\)’. However, it would be a mistake to symbolize sentence 8 with ‘\( \neg H\)’. If Jane is unhappy, then she is not happy; but sentence 8 does not mean the same thing as ‘It is not the case that Jane is happy’. Jane might be neither happy nor unhappy; she might be in a state of blank indifference. In order to symbolize sentence 8, then, we would need a new sentence letter of TFL.

-- **Elaboration**

| Statement              | TFL              | Notes                                  |
|------------------------|------------------|----------------------------------------|
| Jane is happy          | \(H\)            | Atomic proposition                     |
| Jane is not happy      | \(\neg H\)       | Could be unhappy or neutral            |
| Jane is unhappy        | \(U\)            | New atomic proposition (not \(\neg H\)) |

> ✔️ If you're defining "unhappy" as **merely not happy**, then \(\neg H\) is correct.  
> ❌ If you're saying "unhappy" = **specifically sad**, then \(\neg H\) is **not enough** to guarantee that; you need a new atomic sentence, like \(S\) or \( U \) for "sad" or "unhappy".

* However, sad is also not happy so \( \neg H \) is still correct , right? It is valid but not enough to express what you mean by 'Jane is unhappy' if what we mean by 'unhappy' is sad.
    - \( S \rightarrow \neg H \) is valid. But does \( \neg H \rightarrow S \) is valid?
    - Sad implies not happy, but does not happy implies sad? Not happy can imply not sad or neutral emotion also which does not mean Sad or \( S \), right? So we would need a new sentence letter of TFL for that to express it more clearly.


## 5.2 Conjunction

 **Conjunction (AND)**: \( \land \) or \( \& \)  
   - Example: \( P \land Q \) (P and Q)

A sentence can be symbolized as '\( P \land Q \)' if it can be paraphrased in English as ‘Both…, and…’, or as ‘…, but …’, or as ‘although …, …’.
   - Example: \( P \land Q \) (P and Q)

Consider these sentences:

9. ​Adam is athletic.
    - \(A\): ​Adam is athletic.
10. ​Barbara is athletic.
    - \(B\): ​Barbara is athletic.
11. ​Adam is athletic, and also Barbara is athletic.
    - \( ( A \land B ) \)
12. ​Barbara is athletic and energetic.
    - \(B\): ​Barbara is athletic.
    - \(E\): ​Barbara is energetic.  
    - \( ( B \land E ) \)
13. ​Barbara and Adam are both athletic.
    - \( ( B \land A ) \)
14. ​Although Barbara is energetic, she is not athletic.
    - \( ( E \land \neg B ) \)
15. ​Adam is athletic, but Barbara is more athletic than him.
    - Add \(R\) as 'Barbara is more athletic than Adam'
    - \( ( A \land R ) \)

consider:

16. ​Harry stood up and objected to the proposal.
17. ​Harry objected to the proposal and stood up.

If Harry stood up after he objected, sentence 17 is true but sentence 16 is false—the use of ‘and’ here is asymmetric. The symbol ‘
’ of TFL, however, is always symmetric (or “commutative” as logicians say). TFL cannot deal with asymmetric ‘and’. We’ll assume for all our examples and exercises that ‘and’ is symmetric.

Why there's Brackets around the conjunctions? Consider the ff:

18. ​It’s not the case that you will get both soup and salad.
    - \(S1\): You will get soup.
    - \(S2\): You will get salad.
    - \( \neg ( S1 \land S2 ) \)

19. ​You will not get soup but you will get salad.
    - \( ( \neg  S1 \land S2 ) \)

Brackets help us to keep track of things like the scope of the negation.

## 5.3 Disjunction

**Disjunction (OR)**: \( \lor \)  

A sentence can be symbolized as '\( P \lor Q \) if it can be paraphrased in English as ‘Either…, or….’
   - Example: \( P \lor Q \) (P or Q)

Consider these sentences - For these sentences we can use this symbolization key:

- \(F\): Fatima will play videogames
- \(O\): Omar will play videogames
- \(M\): Fatima will watch movies

20. ​Either Fatima will play videogames, or she will watch movies.
    -  \( ( F \lor M ) \) 

21. ​Either Fatima or Omar will play videogames.
    -  \( ( F \lor O ) \) 

It will also help to see how negation interacts with disjunction. Consider:

22. ​Either you will not have soup, or you will not have salad.
    - \(S1\): You will get soup.
    - \(S2\): You will get salad.
    - \( ( \neg  S1 \lor \neg  S2 ) \)

23. ​You will have neither soup nor salad.
    - \( \neg  (  S1 \lor  S2 ) \)

24. ​You get either soup or salad, but not both.
    - \(  (  S1 \lor  S2 ) \land \neg (S1 \land S2 ) \)


## 5.4 Conditional

**Conditional or Implication (IF...THEN)**: \( \rightarrow \) 

A sentence can be symbolized as '\( A \rightarrow B \)' if it can be paraphrased in English as ‘If A, then B’ or ‘A only if B’.
   - Example: \( P \rightarrow Q \) (If P then Q)

Let’s use the following symbolization key:

- \(P\): Jean is in Paris
- \(F\): Jean is in France

25. ​If Jean is in Paris, then Jean is in France.
    - \( P \rightarrow F \)
26. ​Jean is in France only if Jean is in Paris.
    - \( F \rightarrow P \) which is not valid as jean can be in france but not necessary in paris.

In fact, the conditional can represent many English expressions. Consider:

27. ​For Jean to be in Paris, it is necessary that Jean be in France.
28. ​It is a necessary condition on Jean’s being in Paris that she be in France.
29. ​For Jean to be in France, it is sufficient that Jean be in Paris.
30. ​It is a sufficient condition on Jean’s being in France that she be in Paris.

So they can all be symbolized by '\( P \rightarrow F \)'

## 5.5 Biconditional

**Biconditional (IF AND ONLY IF, IFF)**: \( \leftrightarrow \) 

A sentence can be symbolized as '\( A \leftrightarrow B \)' if it can be paraphrased in English as ‘A if⁠f B’; that is, as ‘A if and only if B’.
   - Example: \( P \leftrightarrow Q \) (P if and only if Q)

We will use the following symbolization key:

- \(D\): Laika is a dog
- \(M\): Laika is a mammal

31. ​Laika is a dog only if she is a mammal.
    - \( D \rightarrow M \)
32. ​Laika is a dog if she is a mammal.
    - \( M \rightarrow D \)
33. ​Laika is a dog if and only if she is a mammal.
    - \( (D \rightarrow M) \land (M \rightarrow D) \)
    - \( D \leftrightarrow M \) 

## 5.6 Unless

If a sentence can be paraphrased as ‘Unless,’ then it can be symbolized as '\( (A \lor B) \)'

- \( J \): You will wear a jacket
- \( D \): You will catch a cold

34. ​Unless you wear a jacket, you will catch a cold.
35. ​You will catch a cold unless you wear a jacket.

Both sentences mean that if you do not wear a jacket, then you will catch a cold. With this in mind, we might symbolize them as '\( ( \neg J \rightarrow D) \) '

or '\( ( \neg D \rightarrow J) \) '

or '\( ( J \lor D) \) '

All three are correct symbolizations. Indeed, in *chapter 12* we will see that all three symbolizations are equivalent in TFL.


# 6. Sentences of TFL

In this chapter, we will define exactly what will count as a sentence of TFL. This is one respect in which a formal language like TFL is more precise than a natural language like English?

## 6.1 Expressions

**Definition:**

X is a **Expressions of TFL**, if and only if, it is any string of symbols of TFL. Like the following below:

* Atomic sentences: \( A,  B, C, D,..., Z \)
* with subscripts, as needed: \( A_1, B_1, C_1, A_1, A_{25}, J_{759} \)
* Connectives: \( \neg \land \lor \rightarrow \leftrightarrow \)
* Brackets: \( (,) \)

## 6.2 Sentences

*We want some rules to tell us which TFL expressions are sentences.*

Atomic sentences: \( A,  B, C, D,..., Z \) will count as a sentence

**Formal definition for a sentence of TFL:**

 1. Every sentence letter is a sentence: \( A,  B, C, D,..., Z \) \( A_1, B_1, C_1, A_1, A_{25}, J_{759} \)
 2. If \( \mathcal{A} \) is a sentence, then \( \neg \mathcal{A} \) is a sentence.
 3. If \( \mathcal{A} \) and \( \mathcal{B} \) are sentences, then \( \mathcal{A}  \land \mathcal{B}  \) is a sentence.
 4. If \( \mathcal{A} \) and \( \mathcal{B} \) are sentences, then \( \mathcal{A}  \lor \mathcal{B}  \) is a sentence.
 5. If \( \mathcal{A} \) and \(  \) are sentences, then \( \mathcal{A}  \rightarrow \mathcal{B}  \) is a sentence.
 6. If \( \mathcal{A} \) and \( \mathcal{B} \) are sentences, then \( \mathcal{A}  \leftrightarrow \mathcal{B}  \) is a sentence.
 7. Nothing else is a sentence.

* Definitions like this are called inductive . Inductive definitions begin with some specifiable base elements, and then present ways to generate indefinitely many more elements by compounding together previously established ones. 

* Every sentence is constructed nicely out of sentence letters

* We can find the main logical operator for a sentence by using the following method:
    • If the first symbol in the sentence is ‘¬’, then that is the main logical operator
    • Otherwise, start counting the brackets. For each open bracket, i.e., ‘(’, add 1; for each closing-bracket, i.e., ‘)’, subtract 1. When your count is at exactly 1, the first operator you hit (apart from a ‘¬’) is the main logical operator.
    -  In the case of ‘¬¬¬D’, the main logical operator is the very first ‘¬’ sign. In the case of ‘(P ∧¬(¬Q ∨R))’, the main logical operator is ‘∧’. In the case of ‘((¬E ∨ F) → ¬¬G’, the main logical operator is ‘→’.

* The scope of a connective (in a sentence) is the subsentence for which that connective is the main logical operator.

## 6.3 Bracketing conventions

 Strictly speaking, the brackets in ‘(Q ∧ R)’ are an indispensable or absolutely necessary part of the sentence. Part of this is because we might use ‘(Q ∧R)’ as a subsentence in a more complicated sentence. For example, we might want to negate ‘(Q ∧ R)’, obtaining ‘¬(Q ∧ R)’. 


Second, it can be a bit painful to stare at long sentences with many nested pairs of brackets. To make things a bit easier on the eyes, we will allow ourselves to use square brackets, ‘[’ and ‘]’, instead of rounded ones. So there is no logical difference between ‘(P ∨Q)’ and ‘[P ∨Q]’, for example. Combining these two conventions, we can rewrite the unwieldy sentence

 (((H →I) ∨(I →H)) ∧ (J ∨K))

 rather more clearly as follows:

 [(H →I)∨(I →H) ]︁ ∧ (J ∨K)

 The scope of each connective is now much easier to pick out.

# 7. Ambiguity


* **Ambiguous** , i.e., they can have more than one meaning. 

* **Lexical ambiguity:** a sentence can contain words which have more than one meaning. For instance, ‘bank’ can mean the bank of a river, or a financial institution.
    -  In TFL, we generally aim to avoid ambiguity. -- So, e.g., your symbolization key will need two different sentence letters for ‘Rebecca went to the (money) bank’ and ‘Rebecca went to the (river) bank.’


* **Structural ambiguity:** This arises when a sentence can be interpreted in different ways, and depending on the interpretation, a different meaning is selected. A famous example due to Noam Chomsky is the following:
    - ​Flying planes can be dangerous.

* It is an important feature of sentences of TFL that they cannot be structurally ambiguous. For example:
    - '​Flying planes can be dangerous' can be symbolize as:
        - 1:
            - Let \( A: \) "X is flying in a plane"
            - Let \( D: \) “X can be in danger”
            - Then \( A \rightarrow D \)
        - 2:
            - Let \( F: \) "X is a flying"
            - Let \( P: \) "X is a plane”
            - Let \( D: \) “X is dangerous”
            - Then \( F \land P \rightarrow D \)
    - As we can see, since we can clearly and distinctly define what we mean in TFL, then we can avoid structurally ambiguous in TFL. In other words, each symbol and logical connective has a well and distinctly-defined meaning, leaving no room for different interpretations.

So it depends whether I’m looking for small animals that are dangerous or stealthy (leopard doesn’t count), or whether I’m after either a small, dangerous animal or a stealthy animal (of any size).

## 8.1 Quotation conventions

* Putting quotation marks -- To be clear, the quotation-marks here do not indicate reported speech. They indicate that you are moving from talking about an object, to talking about a name of that object. Eg: ​‘Justin Trudeau’ is the Prime Minister.

## 8.2 Object language and metalanguage

* The language that we are talking about is called the object language .
    -  The object language in this chapter has been the formal language that we have been developing: **TFL**

* The language that we use to talk about the object language is called the metalanguage .
    - The metalanguage is English. Not conversational English exactly, but English supplemented with some additional vocabulary to help us get along.
    - \( A, B, C, A_1, B_1 \) 

* The general point is that, whenever we want to talk in English about some specific expression of TFL, we need to indicate that we are mentioning the expression, rather than using it. We can either deploy quotation marks, or we can adopt some similar convention, such as placing it centrally in the page. Eg: ‣​ ‘D’ is a sentence letter of TFL.

## 8.3 Metavariables

* We also want to be able to talk about any arbitrary sentence of TFL. 

* We used uppercase script letters to do this, namely:

    - \( \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{B}, \mathcal{C} ,,,\)

    - These symbols do not belong to TFL. Rather, they are part of our (augmented) metalanguage that we use to talk about any expression of TFL:

    - ​If \( \mathcal{A} \) is a sentence, then \( \neg \mathcal{A} \) is a sentence.
    
* This talks about arbitrary sentences. If we had instead offered:
    - ​If ‘\(A\)’ is a sentence, then ‘\( \neg A \)’ is a sentence. this would not have allowed us to determine whether ‘\( \neg B \)’ is a sentence. 
    
* To emphasize: ‘\( \mathcal{A} \)’ is a symbol (called a metavariable ) in augmented English, which we use to talk about expressions of TFL. ‘\(A\)’ is a particular sentence letter of TFL.

* The role of the symbol ‘\( \therefore \)’ is simply to indicate which sentences are the premises and which is the conclusion.
