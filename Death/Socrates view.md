# Socrates View About Death

## Socrates Arguments about Death

Below are the formal logical arguments, updated to include direct textual citations and excerpted Stephanus line numbers from Plato’s original texts (*Republic* and *Phaedo*).

---

### Arguments from *Republic Book III*

**The Guardian Courage Argument**

* **Premise 1:** Rulers and guardians of the ideal city must possess the virtue of courage.
* **Premise 2:** Fear of death and the horror of the underworld undermine the virtue of courage.
* **Premise 3:** Stories that depict death as terrifying or wail-worthy instill fear of death in their audience.
> *"We must beg Homer and the other poets not to be angry if we strike out these and similar passages... for depicting the terrors of the world below are untrue and not to be feared by good men."* (*Republic* 3.386b)

* **Conclusion 1:** Therefore, stories depicting death as terrifying or wail-worthy undermine the courage of the guardians.
* **Premise 4:** Anything that undermines the required virtue of the guardians must be prohibited in the ideal city.
> *"Then we must further taboo in our state lamentations and wailings by well-born men."* (*Republic* 3.387d)

* **Final Conclusion:** Therefore, stories that depict death as terrifying or wail-worthy must be prohibited in the ideal city.

**2. The Rational View of Death Argument**

* **Premise 1:** A good man regards his own soul and virtue as more valuable than his physical life.
* **Premise 2:** Loss of a lesser good (physical life) does not warrant excessive lamentation if the primary good (the soul/virtue) remains unharmable.
> *"Then such a man will not think that death is a terrible thing for another such man to undergo... then he will not mourn for him as if he had suffered a terrible fate."* (*Republic* 3.387d–e)


* **Premise 3:** Physical death destroys only physical life, not the soul or its virtue.
* **Final Conclusion:** Therefore, physical death does not warrant excessive lamentation or fear from a good man.

### Part 2: Formal Arguments from *Phaedo*

**1. The Cyclical Argument (Argument from Opposites)**

* **Premise 1:** All process-based generation occurs between pairs of opposite states.
> *"Do not all things which have opposites come to be in this way, and in no other, namely from their opposites?"* (*Phaedo* 70e)


* **Premise 2:** Being alive and being dead are opposite states.
> *"Is not life the opposite of death, as being awake is the opposite of sleeping?"* (*Phaedo* 71c)


* **Premise 3:** If a process goes in one direction between opposites (living to dying), a corresponding reverse process (dead coming to life) must exist to keep nature balanced.
> *"If generation were a straight line, and there were no compensation or circle... all things would finally end in the same state and cease coming into being."* (*Phaedo* 72b)


* **Conclusion 1:** Therefore, a reverse process exists that moves from the state of being dead to the state of living.
* **Premise 4:** A transition from dead to living requires the soul to exist prior to re-entering a body.
> *"The living come from the dead no less than the dead from the living; and this being so, it seems to be a sufficient proof that the souls of the dead must be somewhere."* (*Phaedo* 72a)


* **Final Conclusion:** Therefore, the souls of the dead exist in an unembodied state before coming back into living bodies (reincarnation).

**2. The Argument from Recollection**

* **Premise 1:** Humans possess concepts of absolute perfection (e.g., Equality itself) that do not exist in the imperfect, physical world.
> *"We say there is such a thing as the Equal—not a log to a log, or a stone to a stone... but something beyond all these, Equality itself... We must have known it before we began to see or hear or use the other senses."* (*Phaedo* 74a–75b)


* **Premise 2:** Sensory experience provides only imperfect approximations, which merely trigger memory of these perfect concepts.
* **Premise 3:** If we recollect knowledge upon perceiving physical objects, we must have acquired that knowledge prior to entering this physical world.
> *"If we acquired this knowledge before birth, and lost it at birth, and afterwards by using the senses... recovered the knowledge we had before, is not what we call learning a recovery of our own knowledge?"* (*Phaedo* 75e)


* **Final Conclusion:** Therefore, the soul must have existed in a conscious state prior to entering the physical body.

**3. The Argument from Affinity**

* **Premise 1:** Existence consists of two distinct realms:
* *Realm A:* Physical, visible, changing, and mortal (the body).
* *Realm B:* Non-physical, invisible, unchanging, and immortal (the Forms).
> *"Must we not assume two kinds of existence, the one visible, the other invisible?... The visible is always changing, the invisible always the same."* (*Phaedo* 79a)


* **Premise 2:** The soul is non-physical, invisible, and capable of understanding the unchanging Forms.

* **Premise 3:** Entities that are non-physical and invisible are naturally resistant to decay and dissolution.
> *"The soul is most like the divine, immortal, intelligible, uniform, indissoluble, and unchangeable; while the body is most like the human, mortal, multiform, unintelligible, dissoluble, and perpetually changing."* (*Phaedo* 80b)

* **Final Conclusion:** Therefore, while the physical body decays upon death, the soul naturally resists dissolution and survives bodily death.

**4. The Final Argument (Essential Properties)**

* **Premise 1:** Nothing can admit its opposite without losing its essential identity (e.g., Fire cannot admit Cold and remain Fire).
> *"An opposite can never be opposite to itself... nor can that which brings an opposite ever admit the opposite of what it brings."* (*Phaedo* 103a–105a)


* **Premise 2:** The essential property that the Soul brings to whatever body it inhabits is *Life*.
> *"What is it that, when present in a body, makes it alive? A soul... Then whatever the soul occupies, it always brings life to it?"* (*Phaedo* 105c–d)


* **Premise 3:** The opposite of *Life* is *Death*.

* **Conclusion 1:** Therefore, the soul can never admit *Death*; it is intrinsically *Deathless* (immortal).
> *"The soul does not admit death? No... Then the soul is immortal."* (*Phaedo* 105e)


* **Premise 4:** That which is intrinsically deathless is indestructible.
> *"If the deathless is also imperishable, it is impossible for the soul to perish when death comes upon it."* (*Phaedo* 106b)


* **Final Conclusion:** Therefore, at the moment of bodily death, the soul does not perish; it withdraws intact and immortal.
