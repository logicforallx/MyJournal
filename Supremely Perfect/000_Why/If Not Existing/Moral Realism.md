# Moral Realism

## Definition

**Moral Realism** = The view that moral facts are real and objective, meaning they exist independently of human/someone's opinions, feelings, or beliefs.

Key:

- Real = Not invented by humans.
- Objective = True even if no one believes it.

**Physical World** = The world perceived through the senses as opposed to the mind; tangible or concrete.

**Moral oughts** = It means that doing morally right or good is an obligation.

##  **Moral Realism Argument**  

**P1.** Moral realism requires that moral “oughts” be grounded in something transcendent. Here's why:
1. Moral realism requires moral truths to be real, independent, necessary, and unchanging. *(Base from Definition of moral realism.)*
2. Nothing within the natural/physical world is independent, necessary, or unchanging.*(Our perceived nature is contingent and mutable as we can observe)*
3. Therefore, objective moral truths must be grounded in something transcendent (beyond nature). *(Base from 1 and 2)*

**P2.** If God does not exist, nothing transcendent grounds moral “oughts.”  Why?
- See the documentation in \\Supreme Being\000_Why\If Not Existing\Ontological Ground.md'.

**C1**  If God does not exist, objective moral “oughts” cannot exist. *(Base from 1 and 2)*

**P3.** Without objective moral “oughts,” there can be no genuine moral duties or meaning.  Here's why:
1.  If there are no objective moral “oughts,” then moral duties or meaning collapses into arbitrary social convention or personal choice.
2. If  moral duties or meaning collapses into arbitrary social convention or personal choice, then it is not genuine or objective.

**C2** Hence, without God, moral realism (and moral purpose) cannot actualize and collapses into arbitrary social convention or personal choice. *(Base from C1 and P3)*

## Scrutiny

* Non-theistic moral realism: Philosophers like Derek Parfit, Peter Railton, and Russ Shafer-Landau argue that moral truths can be objective without God—akin to how mathematical truths are objective but not dependent on a deity.
    - Mathematical truths (like 2+2=4) alone and the likes **describe necessary relations** between abstract entities. Moral truths (like "murder is wrong") **prescribe/order obligations** — they are **normative**, not just descriptive. 
    - **Problem:**  
        - *Math* doesn't command or oblige you.  
        - *Morality* **commands** or **oblige**: *you ought not murder*.
        - So morality has an **authoritative** character that math lacks.
    * Math and the likes such as logic, command or oblige someone in the sense that it necessitates someone to act in accordance with it. For if they will not act in accordance with math law or logic law, their plan or action will not succeed. For example, if someone is planning to build a triangle house, it necessitates someone by the law of logic to make 3 sides on that house, does it not?
    - Even if that is the case, why the laws of math, logic or the likes necessitates someone to act morally if doing otherwise is also helpful for them? Like, doing right or good is logically good or right, by implication. But how will it necessitates someone by logic and the likes alone? Will it not succeeds or be fulfilled without following that logic? If God does not exist, then some will ultimately succeeds even if their evil, right?
    - Math/logic can instrumentally obligate (to achieve a goal), but morality claims categorical obligation (binding regardless of goals). Success/failure is goal-relative; morality is not. Does it not?
    - Even if laws of logic, math and the likes necessitates someone to follow that law, those laws (ie., logic, math, etc) necessitates the Existence of Supreme (ie. ontological argument, trademark argument, etc). If those pure logical argument are false, then laws of logic are not entirely reliable, which means that it cannot oblige or necessitates someone entirely, right?

* Moral Platonism: Some posit that moral facts simply exist as part of the fabric of reality, much like numbers or logical truths, requiring no further grounding, as it might be eternal, unchanging, and necessary.
    - Even if those are the case, are ideas or abstract entities alone can actualize something? How do static, non-personal "moral forms" generate duties toward objective beings? How do impersonal abstractions **impose** real obligations on conscious agents?
    - (OPEN)
    ---

* Naturalistic accounts: Others argue that objective moral truths can be grounded in natural facts—for example, facts about human flourishing, suffering, or rational agency.
    - "Natural facts" = "is" statements (e.g., "Pain is unpleasant," "Humans need oxygen."). But an "is" doesn't logically imply an "ought", does it not? (this is David Hume’s **is–ought gap**).
    - (OPEN)
    ---

* The Euthyphro Dilemma: The classic challenge: "Is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good?" If the former, morality seems arbitrary; if the latter, morality is independent of God.
    - This is False dichotomy: It assumes only two options. Third option (Aquinas, Augustine, modern theists): God **is** the Good itself, not external to it, and not arbitrarily inventing it. God's will or nature flow necessarily from It's own Good nature. Therefore, goodness is neither arbitrary nor independent of God.
    - (OPEN)
