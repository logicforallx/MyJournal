# Spinoza’s Argument for God’s Existence

There isn’t a single “exact” wording of Spinoza’s argument for God’s existence because his demonstration appears in his work *Ethics* in a geometric, axiomatic form—and different translators render the text somewhat differently. That said, the central demonstration (found primarily in Part I, Proposition XI of the *Ethics*) can be rendered in a form that captures Spinoza’s original logical structure. (Note: The following is one widely accepted English rendering of the argument; other translations may differ in wording but not in logical content.)


## Spinoza’s Demonstration for God’s Existence

**Preliminaries (Definitions and Axioms):**

1. **Definition of Substance:**  
   *Substance* is “that which is in itself and is conceived through itself” (i.e. it does not require the conception of anything else for its own conception).  
   
2. **Definition of God:**  
   *God* is defined as a substance consisting of infinite attributes, each of which expresses an eternal and infinite essence. In Spinoza’s framework, God is the one and only substance (see also: “Deus sive Natura”).

3. **Axiom (or Principle) Concerning Existence:**  
   In Spinoza’s system, the essence of a substance necessarily involves existence; that is, if something truly qualifies as a substance (in the strict sense), its very definition implies that it exists.

---

**The Argument (Proposition XI – “God exists”):**

- **Step 1: The Nature of Substance**  
  By Definition 1, substance exists in itself and its essence requires that it be self-caused. There can be no substance whose existence is accidental or contingent because that would contradict the idea that substance is conceived through itself.

- **Step 2: The Uniqueness of Substance**  
  Since God is defined (Definition 2) as the only substance—that which possesses infinite attributes—it follows that there can be no other substance besides God. In other words, if there were any other substance, then either our definition of God would be incomplete or there would be a multiplicity of self-caused entities, which Spinoza’s framework does not allow.

- **Step 3: Necessity of Existence**  
  Given the axiom that the essence of substance necessarily includes existence, and since God (as the one substance) has an essence that is infinite and self-contained, God must necessarily exist.  
  If God did not exist, then there would be no self-caused substance, contradicting the very definition of substance and the axiom that existence is inseparable from its essence.

- **Conclusion:**  
  Therefore, by his own definitions and axioms, Spinoza deduces that God (or the one infinite substance) exists necessarily.


## Some Contextual Remarks

- **Geometric Method:**  
  Spinoza famously uses a geometrical method in the *Ethics*—beginning with clear definitions and axioms, and proceeding with propositions and proofs. Proposition XI is the point at which he applies this method to demonstrate the necessary existence of God.  
- **Translation Variations:**  
  Because Spinoza wrote in Latin and later translations into English vary, what is “exact” in one version might differ in phrasing from another. The logical structure, however, remains the same.

## Final Note

While the above is a faithful reconstruction of Spinoza’s argument for God’s existence as presented in his *Ethics*, it’s important to note that what we call “the argument” is embedded in a broader philosophical system. If you require the complete text in its original geometrical order (with every definition, axiom, and proposition as Spinoza presents them), I recommend consulting a reputable edition of *Ethics* (many of which are in the public domain). This rendering captures the logical thrust:
  
- **Substance is self-caused and its essence implies existence.**  
- **God, defined as the infinite substance, therefore necessarily exists.**

This is the heart of Spinoza’s argument—a brilliantly rigorous and uncompromising demonstration that leaves no room for contingency in the existence of God.
