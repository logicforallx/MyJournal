# Unmoved Mover 

## The Unmoved Mover Argument for the Existence of the Supreme (God)

The **Unmoved Mover Argument**, as formulated by Aristotle and later refined by Thomas Aquinas, is a classical argument for the existence of God. Here’s the logical formulation of the argument:

**1. Motion is observable in the world.**  
Everything in the universe is in motion or undergoes change base from observation. "Motion" here refers to the transition from potentiality to actuality.

**2. Whatever is moved is moved by another.**  
Nothing can move itself; rather, it is set in motion by something else. This is based on the Aristotelian principle that potentiality cannot actualize itself without an external cause. This is because, if potentiality can actualize itself without an external cause, then not yet existing as X become existing X by itself (not-tree become tree, not book become book, not human become human, etc by itself). In other words, it is not yet existing as X, but X made (moved) itself to become X, which is contradicting and imply that something comes from nothing, which is absurd as we have documented in '\\Supreme Being\Existence\Ex nihilo nihil fit.md'. For clarification on what they mean for "Nothing Can Move Itself", see below.

**3. This chain of motion cannot regress infinitely.**  
If every moving thing were moved by another in an infinite series, there would be no first mover, and thus no motion at all. Since motion exists, an infinite regress is impossible. This is further justified in '\\Supreme Being\Existence\Infinite Regress.md'.

**4. Therefore, there must be a First Mover that is itself unmoved.**  
To terminate this regress, there must be a being that initiates motion without itself being moved—what Aristotle calls the **Unmoved Mover**.

**5. The Unmoved Mover is what we call God.**  
This First Mover must be pure actuality (having no potentiality to be moved), eternal, necessary, and immaterial. These attributes align with the traditional concept of God.

## **Clarifying "Nothing Can Move Itself"**

At first glance, it seems that some things do move themselves—animals walk, humans think, and even some quantum particles appear to behave without an external mover. However, let’s break it down logically according to Aristotle’s framework. When Aristotle (and Aquinas) say "nothing moves itself," they mean it in a specific **metaphysical** sense. The key is distinguishing between **intrinsic movement** and **extrinsic causation**:

1. **A thing cannot move itself in the sense that its potential cannot actualize itself without an external cause.**  
   - Example: A book on a table does not start moving unless something moves it.
   - Even when something appears to move itself, its ability to move is contingent on prior causes (e.g., biology, physics, energy).

2. **Living beings "move themselves," but only in a secondary sense.**  
   - A person walking seems self-moving, but their movement depends on muscles, nerves, and ultimately biochemical energy derived from external sources (food, air, etc.).
   - These biological processes themselves depend on physical and chemical laws that exist independently of the organism.
   - Therefore, even "self-movers" rely on something prior.

3. **Even quantum particles, despite appearing random, follow laws of probability and quantum fields.**  
   - If quantum fluctuations seem spontaneous, they still occur within a structured framework (quantum fields, conservation laws).
   - They do not violate the principle that every motion (or change) must have some cause.

While some things may appear to move themselves, their ability to do so **still depends on prior causes**—ultimately tracing back to something outside themselves. Aristotle’s argument doesn’t deny that things move **internally**; rather, it asserts that all movement (or change) ultimately requires a foundational cause, which itself is unmoved.

This is why the Unmoved Mover is necessary: without it, you'd have an infinite regress of movers, which would fail to explain why motion exists at all.
