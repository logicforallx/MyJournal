# Why the first cause is the Supremely Perfect Being?



* **--**
1. The universe supports life, beauty, and moral values that allow beings to perceive good and evil.
2. something cannot give what it does not possess.
3. The cause of these positive qualities must itself be the ultimate standard and source of goodness. [1,2]

2. Something cannot give what it does not possess.
3. I am not wholly good to every sentient being.
4. Therefore, the idea of perfect goodness (such as wholly good to every sentient being) came from someone/something that possess that perfect goodness.



* **Argument from Order of Existence**
1. **Premise**: The first cause is the origin of all existence, including the qualities we observe in the world.
2. **Reasoning**: To be the source of all things, the first cause must contain all perfections—**goodness**, **intelligence**, **power**, **beauty**, etc.—to their fullest extent. The first cause cannot be deficient in any way, as it is the source of all things.
3. **Conclusion**: Therefore, the first cause must be **supremely perfect**.

Socrates, as depicted in classical philosophy, often argued dialectically, exploring ideas through question-and-answer dialogue rather than presenting systematic proofs. While Socrates himself did not explicitly present an argument for the "uncaused cause" as a supremely perfect being, we can reconstruct a **Socratic-style argument** based on his method and themes in his philosophical inquiries. Below is a possible Socratic dialogue for this idea:
---
**Socrates**: My dear friend, do you agree that everything that begins to exist must have a cause?  

**Interlocutor**: Yes, Socrates, it seems reasonable. Nothing can come from nothing.  

**Socrates**: Excellent. And among the many things in our world, do we not observe that one thing causes another in an endless chain?  

**Interlocutor**: Certainly. A seed causes a tree, the tree causes fruit, and so on.  

**Socrates**: Tell me, could this chain of causes extend infinitely into the past?  

**Interlocutor**: I suppose it could, though I struggle to imagine it.  

**Socrates**: Consider this: If there is no first cause, then would not the chain itself lack a foundation? How could anything exist at all if there were no ultimate source to initiate the chain?  

**Interlocutor**: That is perplexing. I suppose there must be a first cause to avoid this infinite regress.  

**Socrates**: Indeed. Now, let us consider the nature of this first cause. Must it not, by necessity, be uncaused itself?  

**Interlocutor**: Yes, for if it were caused, it would not be the first.  

**Socrates**: Very good. And tell me, my friend, would such an uncaused cause depend on anything else for its existence?  

**Interlocutor**: It could not, for then it would not be independent.  

**Socrates**: Precisely. Therefore, the first cause must be self-existent and possess its existence by its very nature. Does this not suggest a being of the highest necessity?  

**Interlocutor**: It does, Socrates. But what of perfection?  

**Socrates**: Let us consider. Could a being that lacks power, knowledge, or goodness cause all things, including power, knowledge, and goodness themselves?  

**Interlocutor**: I would think not. The cause must possess these qualities to bring them forth.  

**Socrates**: Then the first cause must be supremely powerful, to account for all power in the universe. It must also be supremely intelligent, to account for the order and design we observe. And would it not also be supremely good, as the source of all goodness?  

**Interlocutor**: That seems logical, Socrates.  

**Socrates**: Tell me, then: if this first cause possesses all power, intelligence, and goodness, and exists necessarily by its own nature, could it be anything less than supremely perfect?  

**Interlocutor**: No, it could not. Such a being would indeed be supremely perfect.  

**Socrates**: And thus, we arrive at the conclusion: the first uncaused cause, the source of all things, must be a supremely perfect being.  

**Interlocutor**: I see now, Socrates. Your reasoning is as clear as sunlight.  

**Socrates**: As always, my friend, I merely ask questions. The truth, as ever, is yours to behold.

---

This dialogue encapsulates the core reasoning Socrates might use: the rejection of infinite regress, the necessity of a first cause, and the logical derivation of perfection from the attributes required of such a cause.
