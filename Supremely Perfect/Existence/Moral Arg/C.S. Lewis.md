# C.S. Lewis's moral argument for the existence of God,

C.S. Lewis's moral argument for the existence of God, as articulated in *Mere Christianity*, can be distilled into a structured syllogism based on his reasoning. Below is a concise presentation of the argument, adhering closely to Lewis's logic and wording:

## **C.S. Lewis's Moral Argument for God's Existence**  

**(As Found in *Mere Christianity*, Book 1: "Right and Wrong as a Clue to the Meaning of the Universe")**

1. **Premise 1 (Universal Moral Law):**  
   All human beings possess an innate awareness of a universal standard of right and wrong, which Lewis terms the "Law of Human Nature" or "Moral Law." This is evidenced by:  
   - The universal practice of appealing to fairness during disputes (e.g., "How’d you like it if someone did that to you?").  
   - The recognition of moral failure (e.g., guilt or shame when violating this standard).  
   - Cross-cultural moral commonalities (e.g., prohibitions against murder, theft, and dishonesty), despite variations in application.  

2. **Premise 2 (Objective, Non-Human Origin of the Moral Law):**  
   This Moral Law cannot be explained as:  
   - **Instinct:** While instincts (e.g., fear, hunger) exist, the Moral Law often *judges* or *directs* instincts (e.g., choosing courage over self-preservation).  
   - **Social Convention:** Societies differ in customs, but all appeal to an objective "ought" (e.g., condemning oppression even if a culture permits it).  
   - **Material Reality:** The Moral Law is not observable in nature (e.g., stones follow gravity, but humans *choose* to obey or defy morality).  

3. **Premise 3 (Necessity of a Lawgiver):**  
   A law presupposes a mind capable of legislating it. Since the Moral Law is:  
   - **Objective** (not contingent on human opinion),  
   - **Universal** (binding on all people),  
   - **Non-physical** (a standard beyond material forces),  
   its source must be a transcendent, intelligent Mind—one that is "more like a mind than anything else we know" (*Mere Christianity*, Bk 1, Ch. 4).  

4. **Conclusion (Existence of God):**  
   Therefore, the Moral Law implies the existence of a Moral Lawgiver, whom we call God. This Being is:  
   - **Supra-natural** (existing beyond the physical universe),  
   - **Personal** (possessing a mind and will),  
   - **Good** (the standard of goodness itself).  

## **Key Lewisian Quotes Supporting the Argument**  
- **On Universal Morality:**  
  "Whenever you find a man who says he does not believe in a real Right and Wrong, you will find the same man going back on [this] a moment later. He may break his promise to you, but if you try breaking one to him he will be complaining ‘It’s not fair’ before you can say Jack Robinson." (Bk 1, Ch. 2)  
- **On the Moral Law’s Transcendence:**  
  "If there is a controlling power outside the universe, it could not show itself to us as one of the facts inside the universe—no more than the architect of a house could actually be a wall or staircase or fireplace in that house." (Bk 2, Ch. 1)  
- **On the Lawgiver’s Nature:**  
  "The Being behind the universe is intensely interested in right conduct—in fair play, unselfishness, courage, good faith, honesty, and truthfulness." (Bk 1, Ch. 5)  

## **Important Notes**  
- Lewis’s argument does **not** conclude with the *Christian* God at this stage; it establishes a generic theistic foundation. His case for Christianity arises later (e.g., through Christ’s claims in Book 2).  
- The argument hinges on the **objectivity** of morality. Lewis rejects relativism by showing that moral disagreements presuppose an objective standard (e.g., arguing about justice assumes justice exists).  

This syllogism captures Lewis’s core reasoning, as he intended in *Mere Christianity*.
