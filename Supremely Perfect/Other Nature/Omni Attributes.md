In "Meditation III" of René Descartes' "Meditations on First Philosophy," Descartes presents a detailed argument for the existence of God, emphasizing various attributes that align with the traditional "omni" characteristics. Here's a closer look at the attributes of God as discussed in Meditation III:

### 1. Omnipotence (All-powerful)

- **Description in Meditation III:** Descartes implies God's omnipotence by discussing the idea that God is the cause of everything and that nothing would exist without God's will. He suggests that God's power is infinite and encompasses all possibilities.
- **Key Quote:** "For I ought to attribute to God all the perfections of which I could have any idea, and I could form no idea of power so great that an infinite power of God does not surpass it."

### 2. Omniscience (All-knowing)

- **Description in Meditation III:** Descartes addresses God's omniscience indirectly by asserting that God must have all the perfections. Since knowledge is a perfection, God must possess complete and infinite knowledge.
- **Key Quote:** "It is no less contradictory to suppose that God, who is supremely perfect, lacks some perfection than to suppose that a mountain lacks a valley."

### 3. Omnibenevolence (All-good)

- **Description in Meditation III:** Descartes touches on God's omnibenevolence by emphasizing that God is not a deceiver. Since deception is a sign of imperfection or malice, and God is supremely perfect, God must be entirely good.
- **Key Quote:** "For since deception is a certain defect, it is clear that it cannot be present in God."

### 4. Immutability (Unchanging)

- **Description in Meditation III:** While not extensively detailed, Descartes' arguments imply God's immutability by emphasizing God's perfection. A perfect being does not change because change would imply a move from perfection to imperfection or vice versa.
- **Key Quote:** This attribute is more implicit in Descartes' emphasis on God’s perfect nature and the stability of God's characteristics.

### Additional Attributes:

- **Infinite:** Descartes discusses the idea of God being infinite, contrasting this with the finite nature of human beings.
  - **Key Quote:** "By the word 'God' I understand a substance that is infinite, independent, supremely intelligent, and supremely powerful, and that created me and everything else."

- **Self-sufficient and Independent:** Descartes argues that God is self-sufficient and the ultimate cause of Himself and everything else.
  - **Key Quote:** "He who has within himself the cause of his own existence possesses in himself every perfection of which he is capable."

### Summary in Meditation III:

1. **Omnipotence:** Descartes infers God’s omnipotence by asserting that God is the ultimate cause of everything.
2. **Omniscience:** Descartes implies God’s omniscience through the necessity of God possessing all perfections, including complete knowledge.
3. **Omnibenevolence:** Descartes explicitly denies that God can be a deceiver, affirming God’s supreme goodness.
4. **Immutability:** Although not extensively detailed, Descartes’ emphasis on God’s perfection suggests immutability.
5. **Infinity:** Descartes discusses God as an infinite being, in contrast to finite human beings.
6. **Self-sufficiency and Independence:** Descartes describes God as the ultimate self-sufficient cause of all existence.

In Meditation III, Descartes uses these attributes to argue for the existence of God, emphasizing that the idea of an infinitely perfect being must have originated from such a being, thus proving God's existence.

#

The argument for the omni-attributes (omnipotence, omniscience, omnibenevolence, omnipresence, and immutability) of a supreme being, particularly in the context of René Descartes and other classical theistic philosophers, stems from a combination of logical deductions, metaphysical considerations, and reflections on the nature of perfection. Here’s a structured argument for why a supreme being must possess these omni-attributes:

### 1. Omnipotence (All-powerful)

#### Argument:
1. **Definition of Supreme Being:** A supreme being is defined as a being than which none greater can be conceived.
2. **Power as Perfection:** Power is a perfection; a being with more power is greater than a being with less power.
3. **Unlimited Power:** A being with limited power can be conceived as having more power. Therefore, a supreme being must have unlimited power to be the greatest conceivable being.
4. **Conclusion:** Therefore, a supreme being must be omnipotent.

#### Reasoning:
- **Logical Deduction:** If any limitation on power exists, then it is possible to conceive a greater being without that limitation, contradicting the definition of a supreme being.

### 2. Omniscience (All-knowing)

#### Argument:
1. **Definition of Supreme Being:** A supreme being is defined as a being than which none greater can be conceived.
2. **Knowledge as Perfection:** Knowledge is a perfection; a being with more knowledge is greater than a being with less knowledge.
3. **Unlimited Knowledge:** A being with limited knowledge can be conceived as having more knowledge. Therefore, a supreme being must have unlimited knowledge to be the greatest conceivable being.
4. **Conclusion:** Therefore, a supreme being must be omniscient.

#### Reasoning:
- **Metaphysical Consideration:** Knowledge encompasses understanding of truth, and a supreme being must fully grasp all truths to be perfect.

### 3. Omnibenevolence (All-good)

#### Argument:
1. **Definition of Supreme Being:** A supreme being is defined as a being than which none greater can be conceived.
2. **Goodness as Perfection:** Moral goodness is a perfection; a being with more goodness is greater than a being with less goodness.
3. **Unlimited Goodness:** A being with limited goodness can be conceived as having more goodness. Therefore, a supreme being must have unlimited goodness to be the greatest conceivable being.
4. **Conclusion:** Therefore, a supreme being must be omnibenevolent.

#### Reasoning:
- **Ethical Reflection:** Perfect moral goodness is essential for a being to be considered maximally excellent and worthy of worship.

### 4. Omnipresence (Present everywhere)

#### Argument:
1. **Definition of Supreme Being:** A supreme being is defined as a being than which none greater can be conceived.
2. **Presence as Perfection:** Presence and influence across all existence is a perfection; a being with greater presence and influence is greater than one with less.
3. **Unlimited Presence:** A being whose presence is limited by space can be conceived as having more presence. Therefore, a supreme being must be present everywhere to be the greatest conceivable being.
4. **Conclusion:** Therefore, a supreme being must be omnipresent.

#### Reasoning:
- **Metaphysical Reflection:** Being present everywhere signifies unlimited influence and sustenance, aligning with the nature of a supreme, sustaining creator.

### 5. Immutability (Unchanging)

#### Argument:
1. **Definition of Supreme Being:** A supreme being is defined as a being than which none greater can be conceived.
2. **Immutability as Perfection:** Immutability is a perfection; a being that does not change is greater than one that does, as change implies potential for improvement or degradation.
3. **Unchanging Nature:** A being subject to change can be conceived as more perfect if it were unchanging. Therefore, a supreme being must be immutable to be the greatest conceivable being.
4. **Conclusion:** Therefore, a supreme being must be immutable.

#### Reasoning:
- **Philosophical Reflection:** Perfection implies completeness and constancy. Any change would imply an imperfection either before or after the change.

### Summary of the Argument:
1. **Supreme Being Concept:** A supreme being is defined as the greatest conceivable being.
2. **Perfection Includes Omni-attributes:** Omnipotence, omniscience, omnibenevolence, omnipresence, and immutability are perfections.
3. **Greatness and Perfections:** To be the greatest conceivable being, the supreme being must possess all perfections in unlimited measure.
4. **Logical Necessity:** Therefore, a supreme being must be omnipotent, omniscient, omnibenevolent, omnipresent, and immutable to fulfill the definition of supreme perfection.

The conclusion that a supreme being must have these omni-attributes follows from the logical and metaphysical necessity that the greatest conceivable being must embody all possible perfections to the fullest extent.


#

The formulation of the omni-attributes of God (omnipotence, omniscience, omnibenevolence, omnipresence, and immutability) can be attributed to a combination of philosophical and theological traditions. Here are the key contributors and the historical context in which these arguments were formulated:

### 1. **Omnipotence (All-powerful)**

#### Key Contributor: Anselm of Canterbury
- **Work:** "Proslogion"
- **Argument:** Anselm's ontological argument for the existence of God implicitly includes the notion of God as the greatest conceivable being, which entails omnipotence.
- **Historical Context:** Anselm (1033-1109) developed the ontological argument in the 11th century, emphasizing that God, being the greatest conceivable being, must have unlimited power.

### 2. **Omniscience (All-knowing)**

#### Key Contributor: Thomas Aquinas
- **Work:** "Summa Theologica"
- **Argument:** Aquinas argued that God, being the first cause and ultimate creator, must have complete and perfect knowledge of all things, both actual and potential.
- **Historical Context:** Aquinas (1225-1274) synthesized Christian theology with Aristotelian philosophy in the 13th century, developing a comprehensive account of God's attributes, including omniscience.

### 3. **Omnibenevolence (All-good)**

#### Key Contributor: Augustine of Hippo
- **Work:** "Confessions" and "City of God"
- **Argument:** Augustine argued that God is the highest good and source of all goodness. His arguments emphasize that God's nature is perfectly good and that all evil is a privation of good.
- **Historical Context:** Augustine (354-430) laid much of the groundwork for Western Christian theology, emphasizing God's supreme goodness in the 4th and 5th centuries.

### 4. **Omnipresence (Present everywhere)**

#### Key Contributor: Gregory of Nyssa
- **Work:** "Catechetical Orations"
- **Argument:** Gregory argued that God's infinite nature means He is present everywhere, sustaining all of creation.
- **Historical Context:** Gregory (c. 335-395), one of the Cappadocian Fathers, contributed to early Christian theology by emphasizing the omnipresence of God in the 4th century.

### 5. **Immutability (Unchanging)**

#### Key Contributor: Boethius
- **Work:** "The Consolation of Philosophy"
- **Argument:** Boethius argued that God, being perfect, cannot change because change implies a move towards or away from perfection.
- **Historical Context:** Boethius (c. 477-524) discussed the nature of God and immutability in the context of classical philosophy in the early 6th century.

### Synthesis by Later Philosophers:

#### René Descartes
- **Work:** "Meditations on First Philosophy"
- **Contribution:** Descartes synthesized these classical arguments, emphasizing that the idea of a supremely perfect being necessarily includes omnipotence, omniscience, omnibenevolence, and immutability.
- **Historical Context:** Descartes (1596-1650) in the 17th century built on medieval theological traditions and used these attributes in his arguments for the existence of God.

### Summary:
- **Anselm of Canterbury:** Omnipotence through the ontological argument.
- **Thomas Aquinas:** Omniscience through causal arguments and theological synthesis.
- **Augustine of Hippo:** Omnibenevolence through discussions of God's nature as the highest good.
- **Gregory of Nyssa:** Omnipresence through the infinite nature of God.
- **Boethius:** Immutability through the nature of perfection.

The omni-attributes of God were not formulated by a single philosopher but rather developed over centuries through the contributions of various thinkers in the classical and medieval periods. Each attribute was examined and integrated into a coherent theological and philosophical system, culminating in the comprehensive understanding we have today.
