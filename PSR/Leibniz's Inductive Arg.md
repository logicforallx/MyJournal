# Leibniz's Inductive Argument 


### Leibniz's Inductive Argument in His Fifth Paper to Clarke

In his correspondence with Samuel Clarke, Leibniz argues inductively for the PSR. He claims that in every instance we encounter, there appears to be a sufficient reason or explanation for a given fact or event. Leibniz argues that since we have found reasons in every known case and have not encountered any counterexamples, the PSR must be universally valid. Let’s explore this inductive reasoning with examples:

1. **Scientific Causation**: In the physical sciences, every observed event or phenomenon is thought to have a cause or explanation. For instance, the boiling of water has a sufficient reason in the form of heat energy being applied to it, which raises the water's temperature beyond its boiling point. We have consistently found that natural phenomena have sufficient causes, leading Leibniz to argue inductively that this should apply universally.

2. **Mathematical Proofs and Principles**: In mathematics, every theorem or derived result is based on axioms, definitions, and previously established theorems. There is a sufficient reason why the result follows from those foundational elements. For example, the sum of the angles in a triangle must equal 180 degrees in Euclidean geometry because of the structure of Euclidean space and the definitions of angles and triangles. There is always a reason why a mathematical truth holds.

3. **Human Actions and Decisions**: Even in human actions, we usually explain a person’s decision by citing their motives, desires, beliefs, or external circumstances. If someone chooses to eat a sandwich, we say that they were hungry, they liked the taste, or they were offered the sandwich. We rarely find actions completely devoid of reasons. The more we study human psychology, the more reasons we uncover behind behaviors.

4. **Historical Events**: Historians seek to explain events by examining causes. If a revolution happens, historians look for economic, political, social, or cultural reasons that sufficiently explain why it occurred. It’s rare (if ever) to conclude that something happened without a reason or cause.

### Why Inductive Reasoning Supports PSR

Leibniz’s inductive argument for the PSR relies on our experience that every fact or event we investigate eventually leads to a sufficient reason. Even if some reasons are initially hidden or complex, we rarely, if ever, encounter something for which there is no cause at all. Because of this track record, Leibniz argues it’s reasonable to extend the principle universally. The PSR becomes not just a metaphysical assumption but a conclusion drawn from our experience of causation.

However, while inductive reasoning makes the PSR plausible, it doesn’t provide an absolute proof. Critics can point out that induction itself cannot guarantee that every fact has a sufficient reason—it only suggests that we haven't found counterexamples so far. This leaves room for skepticism but doesn’t disprove the principle.

### Verdict on Premise 2

In conclusion, premise 2 can be considered true if one accepts that:
- **Reason and experience** consistently show that causes must contain enough reality or power to produce their effects.
- **Inductive reasoning** lends weight to the claim that, in all known cases, facts and events have sufficient reasons, and thus, it’s rational to generalize this principle.

However, one must be willing to embrace the foundational idea that causation requires an equal or greater amount of "reality" or "power" to produce an effect. For those who reject this foundational principle or challenge it with counterexamples, the truth of premise 2 remains debatable. Philosophically, the PSR is powerful but not immune to scrutiny or alternative interpretations of causality..
